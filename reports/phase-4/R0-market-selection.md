# R0 Market Selection and R5-lite Canada Regulatory Sketch: Coffee-Protein Product

Prepared: 2026-10-18 · Desk research only (public web pages; no contact, forms, accounts or purchases) · Owner: Ashish (GTA, Canada) · Budget: CAD 10,000–15,000

> This is not legal advice. Each regulatory line is marked **Confirmed (primary)**, meaning it was read on the regulator's own site, or **Question for counsel**. Market numbers from commercial research firms are low quality and are flagged as such.

---

## 1. Plain-language summary (5 lines)

1. **Start in Canada (GTA plus online across Canada).** It scores highest (3.90/5): you avoid customs, you can sell face to face at gyms, cafés and markets, and a Canadian co-packer can ship to you directly.
2. **A plain coffee + protein drink or powder is probably a regular food in Canada, not a "supplemented food",** as long as the only caffeine comes from the coffee and you add no vitamins, minerals, amino acids or extra caffeine. Have a lawyer or label consultant confirm this before you print labels.
3. **Canada still has real labelling work to do:** labels in English and French, a Nutrition Facts table, the new front-of-pack "high in" symbol (enforced from 1 Jan 2026), protein-claim rules, and a Safe Food for Canadians (SFC) licence for whoever makes, imports or ships across provinces.
4. **The US is the largest market but the hardest to start in.** It has the most competitors, and since Aug 2025 small parcels from Canada need full customs clearance. It is a good second step after Canada.
5. **Leave the UK, EU and Australia for later.** Dairy-protein drinks need animal-product import paperwork, a local importer and expensive freight. None of them can be tested cheaply from the GTA.

## 3. Market notes

### Canada
- **Regulatory.** The Supplemented Foods Regulations (SFR) apply to prepackaged foods with an added "supplemental ingredient" from Health Canada's list: vitamins, minerals, amino acids such as L-leucine, caffeine and others [SRC-R0-02, 03]. Health Canada's SFR guidance lists ready-to-drink protein shakes and protein dry mixes (whey, collagen) as category examples. It does not say that adding protein alone makes a product a supplemented food. RTD coffee-based beverages and instant coffee mixes are permitted SF categories [SRC-R0-04]. The 150 ppm caffeine trigger applies to beverages "with **added** caffeine and a total caffeine content from all sources of more than 150 ppm" [SRC-R0-04]. **Question for counsel:** confirm that a product with only coffee-derived caffeine and whey/plant protein is a regular food and not an SF.
- **Food or natural health product (NHP).** Ready-to-drink beverages in cans or bottles are generally foods. For powders, the deciding factor is how the product is represented. Words like "shake" or "drink" point toward food. Words like "muscle building" point toward an NHP [SRC-R0-10]. Avoid muscle or performance claims on the powder.
- **Landed cost.** A GTA or Ontario co-packer means no customs. Sourcing from a US co-packer would add an import step, which needs an SFC "importing food" licence [SRC-R0-08, 09]. Duty on US-origin product is **Not checked**.
- **Competition.** brüst Protein Coffee Light Roast Cold Brew: 20 g protein, 120 mg caffeine, 18 × 330 mL at Costco.ca [SRC-R0-17]. Brüst is now entering the US at Target and Meijer [SRC-R0-18]. Tim Hortons protein lattes: 20 g hot, 17 g iced, all Canadian locations, Sept 2025 [SRC-R0-19]. Starbucks Protein Lattes and Cold Foam in Canada and the US: about 15–36 g, from Sept 29, 2025 [SRC-R0-20]. Premier Protein Café Latte: 30 g, CA$29.54 per 4-pack on iHerb CA [SRC-R0-21]. Javvy Protein Coffee: US and Canada, July 2025 [SRC-R0-34].
- **Demand.** 71% of Canadian adults drank a coffee beverage "yesterday" in winter 2025 [SRC-R0-22]. Starbucks Canada cites Nourish 2025: 71% of Canadians want to eat more protein (secondary, via a company press release) [SRC-R0-20]. No Canada-specific protein-coffee market size was found (**UNKNOWN**).
- **Test-ability.** Highest of the five. Ashish can sample in person at gyms, cafés, run clubs and markets, and ship by Canada Post within Ontario. Note that the Nutrition Facts table exemption for farmers' markets covers only products sold by the person who made them, so it does not cover a co-packed product [SRC-R0-07].

### United States
- **Regulatory.** FDA does not approve foods before import. Importers need facility registration and prior notice, and must ensure labels meet US rules [SRC-R0-13]. FSVP obligations fall on the US importer [SRC-R0-14]. Caffeine labelling is voluntary for beverages. FDA cites 400 mg/day as generally not associated with negative effects for most adults [SRC-R0-12]. FDA guidance distinguishes beverages (conventional food) from liquid dietary supplements [SRC-R0-35]. **Question for counsel:** whether a powder should be a conventional food or a supplement, and who acts as FSVP importer.
- **Landed cost.** De minimis was suspended for Canadian shipments from 29 Aug 2025. Courier shipments need full clearance. CUSMA duty-free treatment must be claimed on an entry, and postal shipments pay IEEPA tariffs whether or not they qualify under CUSMA [SRC-R0-15].
- **Competition.** The most crowded market: Premier Protein, Starbucks, Javy/Javvy, and Brüst arriving [SRC-R0-18, 20, 21, 34].
- **Demand.** US "protein coffee" valued at USD 2.34B in 2025 by a commercial report, low quality [SRC-R0-23].

### United Kingdom
- **Regulatory.** Under the EU FIC rule, beverages over 150 mg/L caffeine must say "High caffeine content". Beverages based on coffee or tea, with "coffee" or "tea" in the name, are exempt (as quoted by the Finnish Food Authority) [SRC-R0-24]. **Question for counsel:** confirm the GB retained version. Dairy-containing composites need a certificate if they contain processed milk, plus BCP checks unless exempt. Shelf-stable sterilised composites are treated as low risk [SRC-R0-25].
- **Landed cost.** The UK–Canada TCA is in force and CPTPP has applied from 1 Sept 2026, but rules of origin must still be met [SRC-R0-26]. Freight, an importer and port health add cost (ESTIMATED: CAD thousands, not quoted).
- **Competition.** Myprotein × Jimmy's Iced Coffee: 5.6 g protein/100 mL, grocery and convenience [SRC-R0-27]. Caffè Nero × Myprotein protein lattes: 24–25 g, 600+ stores [SRC-R0-28].

### EU (Ireland as entry country)
- Shelf-stable dairy composites from third countries need a private attestation (Annex V, Reg. 2020/2235). The dairy must come from listed countries or establishments, or be UHT-treated or sterilised. Some CN codes (e.g., 2106, 2202) need BCP checks via TRACES unless exempt under Reg. 2021/630 [SRC-R0-29]. **Question for counsel:** Canada's listing status and the CN code for the product.

### Australia
- **Regulatory.** FSANZ P1056 (approved 10 Mar 2026) bans **added** caffeine unless permitted, but naturally occurring caffeine in coffee is not affected. Packaged coffee beverages with ≥200 mg caffeine per serve must declare caffeine in the NIP and carry a "high in caffeine" advisory. There is a 2-year transition from gazettal [SRC-R0-30]. Imports must meet BICON conditions and IFIS inspection [SRC-R0-31].
- **Competition.** Farmers Union Protein Iced Coffee: 36 g per 600 mL, SA/NT [SRC-R0-32]. MaxiNutrition MaxPresso protein coffee powder: 22 g whey, AUD 40 per 300 g, online [SRC-R0-33].

## 5. Recommendation

**Launch first in Canada: in person in the GTA, plus online across Canada.** It is the only market where you can put a can or a scoop in a real customer's hand this month without customs, foreign importers or animal-product import certificates. It also scores far above the others (3.90 vs 2.75 for the US). The competition is real: Brüst is in Costco, and Tim Hortons and Starbucks now sell protein lattes. That shows people want the product, but your angle has to be specific (a flavour, a dairy-free option, a gym or community niche, or a price point). Keep the first recipe simple: coffee, protein and flavour only, with no added vitamins, amino acids or extra caffeine. That probably keeps you outside the stricter "supplemented food" rules. A powder is cheaper and easier to ship and store than a canned drink, so it suits a CAD 10–15k budget well (ESTIMATED; no co-packer quotes were gathered). Before you print a single label, pay for one review by a Canadian food-labelling consultant or lawyer. Confirm the food classification, the bilingual label, the Nutrition Facts table, the front-of-pack symbol and who holds the SFC licence. Treat the US as your step 2, ideally through a US 3PL once you know what sells.
