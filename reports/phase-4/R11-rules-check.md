# R11 – Rules check: sampling a coffee + protein drink in Toronto

> **Not legal advice.** This is desk research from public government web pages, written in plain language for a first-time founder. Rules change and depend on the facts. Before importing product or running a public tasting, confirm with CFIA, Toronto Public Health (TPH), and ideally a food-regulatory lawyer. Anything I could not verify is marked **UNKNOWN**.

Researched 2026-10-20. Scenario: about 60 pouches of white-label protein-coffee powder from a US supplier, served free as tasting samples at daytime sober "coffee rave" and run-club events in Toronto, plus a waitlist sign-up page.

---

## Claim 1: You need an SFC licence to import plus a written Preventive Control Plan (PCP), and you can't rely on the manufacturer's licence

**VERDICT: CORRECTED (partly right). Some exemptions are UNCLEAR, so ask counsel or CFIA.**

- **Licence to import: mostly right.** Under the Safe Food for Canadians Regulations (SFCR), you generally need your own SFC licence to import food. Protein-coffee powder isn't one of the exempt categories, which are food additives, alcohol and some bulk ingredients [SRC-R11-01][SRC-R11-03]. The licence goes to whoever does the importing. Another company's licence (for example, the US manufacturer's) doesn't cover you. A customs broker whose only job is clearing the goods doesn't need one [SRC-R11-03].
- **Free samples still count as "selling."** Both the Safe Food for Canadians Act and the Food and Drugs Act define "sell" to include handing food out "whether or not the distribution is made for consideration." So free samples are legally treated like sales [SRC-R11-05][SRC-R11-06].
- **Written PCP: overstated.** Licence holders whose total food sales over the past 12 months were **$100,000 or less** can be exempt from a *written* PCP. The exemption doesn't cover dairy, eggs, fish, meat, or processed fruit/vegetable products, and it doesn't apply if you ask CFIA for an export certificate. CFIA's own example covers small importers of foods like cookies [SRC-R11-04]. You still have to meet the SFCR's preventive control requirements (sanitation, traceability, and so on); you just don't have to write them up as a formal plan [SRC-R11-04]. **UNCLEAR:** whether a whey/dairy-based powder counts as a "dairy product" under the exclusion. Ask CFIA.

**Exemptions you asked about:**
- **Personal use: does not apply.** It only covers food that is "not for commercial use" and brought in by an individual outside the course of business [SRC-R11-01][SRC-R11-07]. A demand test for a brand is commercial. The personal-use limits are per product, for example 20 kg for coffee/tea/dried foods and 50 L for non-alcoholic drinks. CFIA lists **no dollar (CAD) threshold**; dollar amounts are only for customs duties [SRC-R11-07].
- **Samples / exhibition (100 kg): might apply, but UNCLEAR.** SFCR s. 23(1)(b) exempts food "intended and used for analysis, evaluation, research or a Canadian or international food exhibition" in a shipment of **100 kg or less** [SRC-R11-01]. CFIA's trade-show page says manufactured foods of 100 kg or less need no SFC licence. That only holds if the product is given away for free at a "trade show, exhibition or similar event." Selling it, or handing it out anywhere else, needs CFIA authorization first [SRC-R11-02]. Sixty pouches is probably well under 100 kg. It is **UNCLEAR** whether a run club or coffee rave counts as a "food exhibition or similar event." Get CFIA to confirm in writing before you rely on this. Even when the exemption applies, the importer is still responsible for food safety and for storing and transporting the product properly [SRC-R11-02].
- **A simpler route (not tested with CFIA):** buy from a supplier who is *already* a licensed Canadian importer or distributor. Then you are buying in Canada and not importing yourself. CFIA's guidance says the licence belongs to "the person who imports" [SRC-R11-03]. This option isn't stated directly on these pages, so confirm it.
- **Other points:** the product may also have to meet Canadian labelling rules, and if caffeine is *added* it may be a "supplemented food" (see Claim 4). **UNKNOWN:** whether US-labelled pouches used only for poured samples have to meet Canadian bilingual/Nutrition Facts labelling. Ask CFIA.

## Claim 2: Toronto / O. Reg. 493/17 – samples must come from an inspected kitchen or sealed commercial packs, no home kitchens

**VERDICT: CONFIRMED in substance. Fees are UNKNOWN.**

- **Source of the food.** TPH: "All food sold or given at a special event must be from an approved source," meaning a kitchen inspected by a regulator. "Food produced at home is not allowed" unless that home business has been inspected by a public health unit. Keep purchase records so you can prove where the food came from [SRC-R11-08][SRC-R11-10]. On-site prep should be limited to "final assembly and cooking" [SRC-R11-08]. Mixing powder into drinks at the table would probably count as on-site prep. **UNKNOWN:** whether TPH accepts a US-made product as an "approved source." Ask TPH.
- **What the sampling table needs (from TPH vendor guidance):**
  - **Handwashing.** A self-contained or temporary handwashing station (potable water container with a spigot, waste bucket, liquid soap, paper towels) is required for any on-site food prep. Hand sanitizer alone is only allowed for vendors handling only *pre-packaged, low-risk* food [SRC-R11-08].
  - **Temperature.** Dairy counts as "potentially hazardous." Keep any milk-based or mixed protein drinks at **4°C or colder**, check them with a probe thermometer, and make ice from drinkable water [SRC-R11-08][SRC-R11-11].
  - **Cups and utensils.** "Food vendors should use single-use utensils for food service, sampling, and display" [SRC-R11-08].
  - **Certified food handler.** At least one is required at the booth at all times [SRC-R11-08][SRC-R11-11].
- **Permits, notice and lead time.** The *event organizer* must notify TPH at least **30 days** before the event (Special Event Notification form, then a Food Safety Management Plan) [SRC-R11-09][SRC-R11-12]. *Vendors* fill in the organizer's vendor package and special event forms at least **three weeks** before the event [SRC-R11-08]. **Fees: UNKNOWN.** None of the TPH pages I checked lists a fee.

## Claim 3: Is an Ontario licence or permit needed just to hand out free non-alcoholic samples at someone else's event?

**VERDICT: CONFIRMED (the organizer holds the main obligation), with caveats. Parks may be the exception.**

- **No provincial licence.** The AGCO regulates only alcohol, lottery/gaming, cannabis retail and horse racing, so non-alcoholic samples need no AGCO licence [SRC-R11-13]. I found no separate Ontario or Toronto permit for a vendor at a special event. You would be one of the organizer's food vendors under TPH rules: their notification, plus your vendor package [SRC-R11-08][SRC-R11-09].
- **Parks caveat.** Run clubs often meet in City parks. Toronto's Parks by-law bans selling or offering food or drink, carrying on a business, and handing out flyers or other "advertising devices" in a park "unless authorized by permit" [SRC-R11-14]. It doesn't mention *free* samples directly, so whether free sampling in a park needs a permit is **UNCLEAR**. Ask the organizer whether they hold a park permit that covers you, or ask Parks.
- **Not relevant:** Toronto's marketing display permit covers selling goods on City sidewalks outside a store, so it likely doesn't apply here [SRC-R11-15].

## Claim 4: Caffeine/protein claims on a "coming soon" landing page or flyer

**VERDICT: CONFIRMED that the rules apply before launch. The details are UNCLEAR, so ask counsel.**

- **Ads count, not just labels.** Nutrient content claim rules (for example "source of protein" or "high in protein") apply to labels *and* advertisements, and protein claims have to meet set criteria [SRC-R11-16]. The SFC Act bans advertising food in a way that is "false, misleading or deceptive" [SRC-R11-06]. Under the Competition Act, the "general impression" of an ad is what counts, and performance claims need proper tests to back them up *before* you make them [SRC-R11-17]. I found no pre-launch exemption.
- **Caffeine.** If caffeine is *added*, the product is likely a "supplemented food." Since 1 January 2026 those need a Supplemented Food Facts table and may need a caution identifier [SRC-R11-18]. **Practical tip:** use only numbers you can show on the final spec sheet (for example "~X mg caffeine, Y g protein per serving – final values TBC"). Avoid health or energy-benefit claims, and don't call it "high protein" until it meets the criteria.

## Claim 5: CASL consent wording for a waitlist sign-up page

**VERDICT: CONFIRMED (consent is needed). Here are the exact elements.**

- **What the consent request must say.** Under the CRTC regulations it must clearly state: (a) the purpose (for example "to email you product updates and launch offers"), (b) your business name, (c) your mailing address plus a phone number, email or web address, and (d) that people can withdraw consent at any time [SRC-R11-19][SRC-R11-20].
- **How consent is given.** Consent has to be opt-in. **No pre-checked boxes**: the person must tick the box or type their email themselves [SRC-R11-21]. Keep records of when and how each person consented [SRC-R11-22].
- **What each email must include.** Every email you send needs your identity, contact information valid for at least 60 days, and a working unsubscribe link. Unsubscribes must be honoured within 10 business days [SRC-R11-22][SRC-R11-20].
- **Sample wording:** "☐ Yes, email me updates and launch offers from [Brand name], [mailing address], [email]. You can unsubscribe at any time." **UNKNOWN:** whether a PO box works as the "mailing address." Check with the CRTC FAQ or counsel.
